[doorplate](https://github.com/jeremeybingham/doorplate) 0.5.1 is out. It is still an alpha: an open-source US address parser written in Rust, with a
[Python package](https://pypi.org/project/doorplate/) and a [command-line tool](https://crates.io/crates/doorplate-cli). There was no post for
0.5.0, which changed the match key to `k4` and made the parser a little slower. 0.5.1 is the release that fixes the slowness, so this post covers both:
what the `k4` release changed and how I checked it, and how the same parser now does a parse in half the time of 0.4.1 with no key changing.

The graphic above is the short version. Its accuracy bars are the benchmark suite and the geocode benchmark against usaddress 0.5.16 (4 CPUs, 8 GB), and usaddress
still wins on its own test files, 100% to 99.1%, which is why the graphic shows that row. The "2x faster than 0.4.1" comes from the engine measurement
further down, on one pinned CPU.

*doorplate parses and formats addresses. It does not validate that an address exists or can receive mail.*

## What changed since 0.4.1

- **The match key is `k4`** (0.5.0). The layout is the same as `k3`; the value of some keys is different, because the parser reads more inputs correctly.
  Keys of different versions never compare equal: store the `k4:` prefix with your keys and re-parse the original addresses to re-key. Against the
  0.4.0 keys, 127,869 of 36,920,703 keys change (0.35%) across seven public data sets. **0.5.1 changes no key:** I compared keys, unit fields and
  warnings row by row against 0.5.0 on the same seven sources and found no difference.
- **More messy shapes are read.** A name or recipient text around the street; a box first and a street after it; `PO` with no `BOX`; `NO.9` and a unit
  after a street suffix; a `#` before a designator; a spaced hyphen inside a unit; a letter after the house number (`46 A LINDEN AVE` is `46A`); a half written
  without its slash (`31 1 2 ELM PLACE` is `31 1/2`); a street line written twice; care-of text in front; a nearly right ZIP; a campus mailbox. The list, with the
  count each rule changed, is in the [changelog](https://github.com/jeremeybingham/doorplate/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md).
- **Three new warnings:** `repeated_text`, `zip_repaired` and `non_us_address`.
- **Half the time per parse** (0.5.1). More below.
- **Parse a CSV file in the browser.** The [Try doorplate page]({{ site.baseurl }}/demo/) now takes a CSV file, lets you pick the address column or the line 1,
  line 2, city, state and ZIP columns, and gives you the parsed file back. It runs the library compiled to WebAssembly in your browser; nothing is uploaded.
- **The documentation was edited** for a reader of the current version: the user pages no longer carry issue numbers and release history, and the
  accuracy and speed pages show the current figures.

## How I checked the key change

Every rule was merged on its own, measured against the frozen keys of the version before it, and has an invented corpus case. For the release I added two
measurements that run over everything at once, on the old build (0.4.1) and on the new one:

- **A probe for every open issue.** A probe is a text pattern for a known problem, for example "a PO box with a number after a street". Counted over the
  seven large sources (36.9 million rows) it says how common the shape is and how many of those rows the parser flags. 35 of 35 open rule issues have a probe
  or a written reason why not. The probes of fixed problems should fall, and these did (rows flagged on a 1-in-10 sample, scaled):

  | Shape | Flagged before | Flagged after |
  |---|---|---|
  | a box after a street number | 11,520 | 2,870 |
  | a PO box number equal to the ZIP | 7,440 | 280 |
  | a `#` right before a designator | 7,300 | 200 |
  | a level word alone on a line | 4,490 | 1,420 |
  | `PO` and a number, no `BOX` | 3,030 | 630 |
  | a spaced hyphen inside a unit | 2,320 | 330 |

- **A full run over 146.6 million Netsyms addresses**, on both builds: rows flagged 1,888,824 to 1,887,446, and `unparsed_remainder` (text the parser could not
  place) 812,981 to 764,516. The run also showed 24,770 rows with the new `repeated_text` warning.

The benchmark suite agrees: the whole suite goes from 92.3% to 93.4% of addresses right, the `hard` stratum from 54.6% to 61.8%. The Florida one-line check and a
comparison of cities on four geocode splits did not move at all.

## How the speed came back

0.5.0's new rules added passes over every input, and `parse` was 7% to 9% slower than in 0.4.1. I accepted that for the key release and took it back in
0.5.1 without touching a key. On the suite's `natural` mix on one pinned CPU, a parse took 31.1 µs in 0.4.1, 33.3 µs in 0.5.0 and takes 15.6 µs now.
Counted in instructions (which repeat exactly, where wall time on my machine does not) it is about 157,500, 171,600 and 85,900 per parse.

![Time per parse for 0.4.1, 0.5.0 and 0.5.1: 31.1, 33.3 and 15.6 microseconds on one pinned CPU; 157.5, 171.6 and 85.9 thousand instructions]({{ site.baseurl }}/assets/doorplate-0-5-1-speed.png)

*The chart shows the 0.5.0 dip and the recovery. Both charts are on the benchmark suite's everyday addresses (`natural`), measured with the three builds side by side in one
session; the instruction counts repeat exactly, the wall times do not (on my machine they move by 10% to 60% from one day to the next), so read the ratios and not the last digit.
The bars start at zero, and nothing else changed: the parser's output is the same as 0.5.0's.*

What worked was mostly about not asking questions twice, and not asking questions whose answer is no:

- **83% of the table lookups find nothing.** Each table (suffixes, units, states, places and so on) now has a small bit filter that answers "not in the table"
  without the binary search. That alone gave about 19% more parses per second, and the wall time fell more than the instruction count because a binary search
  mispredicts its branches.
- **Rule out a word by its first two bytes** before normalizing it into a key, and test whether any multi-word entry starts with a word before joining
  runs of words to look for one.
- **Do work only when it can matter:** find the cut in a glued city before running the checks that can only veto it (a 12% gain on its own), check a ZIP only
  when the state could also be a street suffix, and skip the punctuation readings for a token that is only letters and digits.
- **Build the release with fat link-time optimization** (3%).

What did not work is worth saying too. A cache of lookup results per token removed none of the lookups, because the tagger asks about each token about once. A
branch-free rewrite of the key normalizer was 2% slower, because most words that are not keys leave the loop at the first byte. Cutting the number of calls
worked where rewriting a hot function did not.

From Python the gain is smaller, because the work of building result objects is not the parser: `parse` on the corpus inputs went from 63,901 per second in
0.4.1 to 72,755 now. On the benchmark suite's inputs, against usaddress on one thread, doorplate does
43,411 addresses per second to its 9,297, and 137,985 with `parse_many` on 4 threads. The [speed page](https://github.com/jeremeybingham/doorplate/blob/main/docs/speed-and-size.md)
has the table, the method and the dead ends.

## What is still worse

- **One benchmark dips.** On the geocode benchmark's dev split the score fell from 94.5% to 94.3% in 0.5.0 (113 of 60,522 strings read differently). At least 92 of
  them are one rule: a letter A to D after the house number now joins the number, which the reviewed data sources do and that benchmark's labels do not. I think
  the rule is right, but I have not settled it with a review of real rows.
- **Two known misreadings** found by the key check: a compass word followed by `KY` or `TR` and a number (`EAST TR 56`) is read with a suffix (about 120 rows in
  37 million), and a campus box with two plain words before `BOX` is not recognized. Both are issues.
- **It is an alpha.** The `k4` is new and has only been checked on the project's own data (public records). A `k5` is planned for an eventual 0.6.0 release, so
  keep the version prefix with the keys you store.

## What is next

More speed is possible (memory allocation is still about a sixth of the instructions), but I stopped here: the remaining gains are smaller and each one costs
more. Parse rules that surface from here go through the same loop (a review of real rows, a count, an invented case) and wait for
the `k5`, so that stored keys change as rarely as possible.

Still wanted most: real, messy addresses. `doorplate anonymize` ([0.4.1]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/10/28/doorplate-0-4-1.html)) keeps the shape of an address and changes the digits and names, so you can send some
without sending the addresses.

```sh
pip install -U doorplate
```
