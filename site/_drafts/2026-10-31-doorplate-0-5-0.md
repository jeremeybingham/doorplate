[doorplate](https://github.com/jeremeybingham/doorplate) 0.5.0 is out. It is still an alpha: an open-source US address parser written in Rust, with a
[Python package](https://pypi.org/project/doorplate/) and a [command-line tool](https://crates.io/crates/doorplate-cli). This release is the second
time I have changed what a stored key means, so this post is about that, and about how I tried to be sure the change is the one I meant.

*doorplate parses and formats addresses. It does not validate that an address exists or can receive mail.*

## What changed

- **The match key is now `k4`.** The layout is the same as `k3`; the value of some keys is different, because the parser reads more inputs correctly. Keys of
  different versions never compare equal: store the `k4:` prefix with your keys and re-parse the original addresses to re-key. Against the 0.4.0 keys,
  127,869 of 36,920,703 keys change (0.35%) across seven public data sets.
- **More messy shapes are read.** A name or recipient text around the street; a box first and a street after it; `PO` with no `BOX`; `NO.9` and a unit
  after a street suffix; a `#` before a designator; a spaced hyphen inside a unit; a letter after the house number (`46 A LINDEN AVE` is `46A`); a half written
  without its slash (`31 1 2 ELM PLACE` is `31 1/2`); a street line written twice; care-of text in front; a nearly right ZIP; a campus mailbox. The list, with the
  count each rule changed, is in the [changelog](https://github.com/jeremeybingham/doorplate/blob/main/CHANGELOG.md).
- **Three new warnings:** `repeated_text`, `zip_repaired` and `non_us_address`.

## How I checked it

Every rule was merged on its own, measured against the frozen keys of the version before it, and has an invented corpus case. For the release I added two
measurements that run over everything at once, on the old build (0.4.1) and on the new one:

- **A probe for every open issue.** A probe is a text pattern for a known problem, for example "a PO box with a number after a street". Counted over the
  seven large sources (36.9 million rows) it says how common the shape is and how many of those rows the parser flags. 35 of 35 open rule issues have a probe
  or a written reason why not. The closed issues' probes should fall, and these did (rows flagged on a 1-in-10 sample, scaled):

  | Fixed issue | Shape | Flagged before | Flagged after |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | #299, #300, #301 | a box after a street number | 11,520 | 2,870 |
  | #260 | a PO box number equal to the ZIP | 7,440 | 280 |
  | #340 | a `#` right before a designator | 7,300 | 200 |
  | #292 | a level word alone on a line | 4,490 | 1,420 |
  | #305 | `PO` and a number, no `BOX` | 3,030 | 630 |
  | #309 | a spaced hyphen inside a unit | 2,320 | 330 |

- **A full run over 146.6 million Netsyms addresses**, on both builds: rows flagged 1,888,824 to 1,887,446, and `unparsed_remainder` (text the parser could not
  place) 812,981 to 764,516. The run also showed 24,770 rows with the new `repeated_text` warning.

The benchmark suite agrees: the whole suite goes from 92.3% to 93.4% of addresses right, the `hard` stratum from 54.6% to 61.8%. The Florida one-line check and a
comparison of cities on four geocode splits did not move at all.

## What got worse

- **It is slower.** `parse` is 8 to 12% slower than in 0.4.1 (about 56,000 addresses per second against 64,000 on the corpus inputs, one thread). The new rules add
  passes over every input. 0.5.x is for getting that back, without changing a key.
- **One benchmark dips.** On the geocode benchmark's dev split the score falls from 94.5% to 94.3% (113 of 60,522 strings read differently). At least 92 of them are
  one rule: a letter A to D after the house number now joins the number, which the reviewed data sources do and that benchmark's labels do not. I think the rule is
  right, and I have filed a review queue to settle it rather than decide it from my own data.
- **Two known misreadings** found by the check: a compass word followed by `KY` or `TR` and a number (`EAST TR 56`) is read with a suffix (about 120 rows in 37 million), and
  a campus box with two plain words before `BOX` is not recognized. Both are issues.

## What is next

0.5.x is performance. The numbers above are the baseline. Parse rules that surface from here go through the same loop (a review of real rows, a count, an invented
case) and wait for the next key version, `k5`, so that stored keys change as rarely as possible.

Still wanted most: real, messy addresses. `doorplate anonymize` ([0.4.1]({{ site.baseurl }}/2026/10/28/doorplate-0-4-1.html)) keeps the shape of an address and changes the digits and names, so you can send some
without sending the addresses.
